Hetzner Impersonated, 23,000 China Mobile Prefixes Hijacked: Why Didn't RPKI Stop It?​

Between June and August 2026, the routing system recorded two large-scale BGP hijacks.

Event A: the attacker forged the origin of Hetzner (AS24940) and announced, inside 162.55.0.0/16, a previously unannounced 162.55.80.0/24. A single prefix, active for 32.8 hours, with a peak of 74.9% of vantage points seeing the hijacked route.

Event B: the attacker forged the origin of China Mobile, affecting a cumulative 23,390 prefixes in two months — 78% of all prefixes announced by China Mobile.

Notably, the attack routes in both events passed validation: Event A's /24 fell inside the maxLength authorized by the victim's parent ROA; in Event B neither the prefixes nor their origin were altered, and 93.7% of the hijacked prefixes validated as valid.

RPKI is deployed — so why did the hijack still succeed?

Figure 1 | Overview of the two events

01  Event A: A More Specific /24

162.55.80.0/24 was not announced before the incident. The attacker AS62390 (NexonHost, Romania) originated it with AS24940 as origin — the parent ROA allows maxLength 24, so origin validation passed. Even with the legitimate /16 announced at the same time, longest prefix match still let the more specific /24 win.

The route first appeared at 20:57, reaching 480 vantage points within about a minute, peaking at 507/677 (74.9%). All 1,445 distinct hijacked paths entered the global routing table via 62390 → 6204; among several upstreams only Orange and GTT actually carried it, and seven Tier-1 networks never forwarded it.

At 08:44 on 08-29, Hetzner self-originated the /24, and within about six minutes the hijack share fell from 75% to nearly 0 (99.6% of vantage points switched back to the legitimate route). The attacker resumed for about ten hours that night; after Hetzner re-originated once more the next day, the event ended.

Figure 2 | Each time Hetzner self-originated the /24, the hijack share fell from 75% to 0 within 6 minutes

Figure 3 | How Tier-1 ASes handled the hijack announcement (red = ASes that accepted and forwarded the hijacked route)

Afterwards Hetzner tightened the relevant ROAs and added ASPA records. One easily overlooked detail: across the 511 vantage points where both routes coexisted, the hijacked path was on average 2.80 hops longer — but under longest prefix match, path length does not matter.

Figure 4 | Hijacked paths were on average 2.80 hops longer than legitimate paths (all 511 paired vantage points longer)

The impact went beyond the routing layer: the prefix hosted Softaculous update and billing servers; the attacker hijacked the update endpoint, obtained a valid TLS certificate, and delivered a malicious Virtualizor update package to some customers.

02  Event B: A Shorter AS_PATH

Event B needed no more-specific prefix. The attacker AS402425 (QOREXA LABS, US) kept the victim prefixes and the legitimate origin unchanged and altered only AS_PATH. Four phases:

P1 rehearsal (6/15–7/7, 27 prefixes): AS402425 itself as origin; origin validation returned invalid, visibility near zero; abandoned;

P2 small scale (7/9–7/24, 135 prefixes): switched to path hijacking with a forged China Mobile origin, alongside DNS hijacking;

P3 large scale (7/25–8/17, about 9,000 prefixes): Hurricane Electric notably permitted it, visibility peaking at 94.3%;

P4 rotation (8/18–8/31, about 20,000 prefixes): upstream switched to Cogent, short-lived prefix rotation; on 8/19 alone it announced 19,848 distinct prefixes.

Figure 5 | Daily count of prefixes whose AS_PATH contains AS402425 (grouped by origin)

Figure 6 | The four hijacked paths for 120.197.0.0/16 (original figure labels are in Chinese)​

China Mobile's ROA practice is itself strict (maxLength matches the actual announced length), and 96.7% of all its announced prefixes pass origin validation. But origin validation only answers whether the prefix is authorized to that origin; it does not verify whether each adjacency in AS_PATH actually exists.

At vantage points that observed both routes, the attack path was on average 1.38 hops shorter than the legitimate path back to China, and about 88% of vantage points chose the attack path — the shorter AS_PATH won. Traffic returning to China was drawn to the attacker side via HE and Cogent and used for DNS answer tampering.

Figure 7 | Hijacked paths were 1.38 hops shorter than legitimate paths (88% of vantage points shorter)

The attacker also claimed more than 13 China Mobile provincial ASNs as its own “customers”; under the backbone AS9808 alone it claimed to carry 14,187 prefixes. Current mechanisms require no authorization between an origin and its upstream, so such a claim stands without any credential.

03  Shared attack logic

What both events exploited is not an implementation flaw but the semantic boundary of origin validation:

1. Make the route “legitimate” first: Event A used maxLength to cover a never-announced /24; Event B kept a legitimate origin and forged only the path relationship.

2. Then make the route “better”: A won longest prefix match with a more specific prefix; B won path selection with a shorter AS_PATH.

3. Propagation depends on a few upstreams: A was actually carried only by Orange and GTT; B depended on HE and Cogent. The effect depends not only on the attacker's capability but also on whether a few networks let an anomalous route through.

4. Response speed determines the loss: Hetzner suppressed propagation within six minutes by re-originating; Event B lasted two and a half months.

The victim's own configuration matters just as much: of Hetzner's 62 validated announcements, 51 could be broken by the same technique. 22.4% of valid announced prefixes worldwide are in the same state; and even a strict ROA operator like China Mobile cannot stop path forgery.

04  Recommendations

Tighten ROA authorization. Keep maxLength equal to the longest prefix actually announced, and avoid fallback patterns such as /16 → maxLength 24. Hetzner tightened some of its ROAs afterwards, but 50 remain unchanged.

Deploy ASPA. ASPA takes effect as soon as the side whose origin was forged signs it: it constrains provider relationships in AS_PATH and complements origin validation. As of 17 September 2026, about 3,112 ASes worldwide have signed ASPA records (2.98% of origin ASes); the key is that major propagation networks such as HE and Cogent actually perform the check.

Watch for combined anomalies. A newly appeared ASN plus a large number of non-owned prefixes in a short time plus high churn (Event B used rotation to evade static thresholds) should drive alerts that combine AS_PATH features, prefix counts and uptime.

Prepare a response plan. Pre-stage a re-origination plan for critical prefixes that takes effect within minutes, and establish security contact channels with major transit providers and IX peers.

Conclusion

Origin validation can constrain who is entitled to be the origin of a prefix, but it cannot by itself prove that a BGP path comes from legitimate network relationships. Once an attacker constructs an origin whose authorization holds, prefix length and AS_PATH still provide two independent attack paths.

RPKI-valid should be understood as origin authorization valid, and should not be equated with route provenance valid.
